# Marketing & customer retention mechanics

> Researched 2026-08-05, in response to: "we defently need to introduce marketing
> concepts... how to advertise myself, should i really only rely on my product, if so what
> makes people come back to buy more?"

## What the research found

- Gamified loyalty programs measurably lift real-world retention (~22%) and engagement
  (~48%) versus non-gamified equivalents — but only when tied to **genuine behavior**
  (points/tiers/challenges for real actions), not vanity metrics.
- Tycoon/business-sim design principle: these games are explicitly **customer-oriented** —
  the core loop revolves around what customers find worthwhile, and customers need
  independent-feeling behavior (their own quirks/preferences), not a single global demand
  number, or the "market" reads as a dial instead of people.
- Post-purchase engagement mechanics in commercial gamification (points, missions, VIP tiers,
  referral incentives) exist specifically to give a customer "a reason to stay engaged
  between purchases" — i.e., retention is designed as its own mechanic, not assumed to follow
  automatically from product quality.

## HUSTLE application

This directly answers the "should I only rely on my product" question mechanically:

- **Split "demand" into two visible things**: raw market demand (already exists) and a new
  **repeat-customer / reputation** signal that only moves in response to specific choices —
  a marketing-spend allocation, consistently good service on busy days, or a Stage 3 plan
  answer about who the target customer actually is. A player who has strong product-market
  fit but zero marketing visibly plateaus lower than one who balances both — same for the
  reverse (heavy ad spend, weak fundamentals).
- **Tie this to the budgeting mechanic** (see `porters-five-forces-and-strategy.md`): treating
  marketing as a real line-item in a constrained budget, not a separate side-system, is what
  makes "should I spend on ads or save the buffer" a genuine tradeoff instead of a free action.
- **Customers as more than a demand dial**: even a lightweight touch — e.g. one or two named
  recurring customers who show up across multiple Crisis days with their own small
  preferences — would make "repeat business" feel like a relationship instead of a stat,
  matching the tycoon-design principle that customers need independent behavior to read as
  real.
